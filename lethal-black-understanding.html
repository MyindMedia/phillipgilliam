Core Thesis

Deny knowledge. Deny wealth. Deny protection. Deny power. Then punish resistance.

From the first enslaved Africans brought into what became the United States in 1526, the system changed forms over time. The pattern never did. To be crystal clear, there were no "slaves" stolen from Africa. America enslaved scientists, farmers, mathematicians, philosophers, doctors, legal minds and multilingual translators.

01

They Arrived With Knowledge

Rice cultivation, ironworking, medicine, navigation, mathematics, oral law, Islamic scholarship. Carolina rice was profitable because of an entire West African technical system, extracted and never credited.

02

Learning Made Illegal

Mocked for not speaking English, then punished for trying to learn it. After Nat Turner's 1831 revolt, most slave states outlawed teaching enslaved people to read or write.

03

Then Called Ignorant

Black expertise built white wealth while the ledger, the court, the church and the schoolhouse refused to count it as education. Chains to slave codes to Jim Crow to redlining to mass incarceration. The method changed. The objective did not.

"America took brilliant Africans, stole their labor and knowledge, stripped them of language and family continuity, outlawed their education, exploited their science, and then called them ignorant for not being educated inside the very system that criminalized their learning."

Inside the Book

Six systems of violence.
One throughline.

Each system is summarized, then backed by a source-support section citing the Smithsonian, the Equal Justice Initiative, Mapping Inequality, the Federal Reserve, the USPTO and more.

01

Educational Violence

Making Black learning illegal, inferior, or underfunded. Anti-literacy laws, segregation academies, discriminatory discipline, and the school-to-prison pipeline.

02

Political Violence

Denying the vote. Black Codes, poll taxes, white primaries, Shelby County, Brnovich, and the 2026 Louisiana v. Callais decision that gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act.

03

Economic Violence

Stolen labor, blocked property, credit denial and wealth extraction. Sharecropping, convict leasing, redlining, and the GI Bill that white families cashed and Black veterans could not.

04

Justice-System Violence

Slave patrols to mass incarceration. Vagrancy laws, lynching with legal impunity, cash bail, drug-war disparities, felony disenfranchisement, prison labor.

05

Housing, Health & Environmental Violence

Restrictive covenants, urban renewal, highway construction, appraisal bias, and the durable racial geography that decides who breathes clean air and who lives near the hospital.

06

Cultural & Historical Violence

Narrative control. Slavery taught as benign, massacres erased, ethnic studies attacked, books banned, DEI demonized, and Black advancement framed as favoritism rather than repair.

The 1858 law that
erased Black genius.

In 1858 the U.S. Attorney General's opinion in Invention of a Slave held that an enslaved inventor could not receive a patent. He could not take the required legal oath or own the right. The slaveholder could not patent it either, because he was not the inventor. Enslaved Africans could create useful technology, and the law denied them authorship.

The book traces that injury forward: Henry E. Baker's recovered lists of Black patent holders, the role models missing from the curriculum, the "Lost Einsteins" research, and the racial wealth gap that widens every time a Black idea is commercialized by someone else.

It closes with a case the author knows firsthand. Phillip Gilliam invented and patented Stored Elastic Energy Transfer (U.S. Patent 5,957,878), the biomechanics behind the Angle Shoulder Brace worn across the NFL, NBA, NHL and NCAA. The exhibits document what he describes as the appropriation of that design without an agreement, and the litigation funding that never arrived before the window closed.
